\subsection{Quantum Computing Preliminaries}
\label{sec:quantum-preliminaries}

In quantum computing, a \emph{qubit} is the fundamental unit of information. Its state is represented as a linear combination (i.e., superposition) of the two basis states $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, namely $\ket{\psi} = \alpha \ket{0} + \beta \ket{1}$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex amplitudes satisfying $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.
Quantum computation proceeds by applying \emph{quantum operators} $U$, which are unitary operators that evolve the system state according to $\ket{\psi_{t+1}} = U \ket{\psi_t}$. Since unitary operators are invertible, applying $U^\dagger$ makes it possible to retrieve the initial state.
In this work, we represent quantum computations using the \emph{circuit formalism}. In this representation, a qubit is depicted as a horizontal wire, while quantum operators are represented as gates applied to these wires, acting on single or multiple qubits. The time evolution is conventionally read from left to right. An example of such a representation is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:c-to-circuit}.
For clarity, qubits are often logically grouped into \emph{quantum registers} of arbitrary size. Likewise, quantum gates may operate on individual qubits or represent more complex multi-qubit subroutines.
Any quantum gate can be realised using a finite sequence of gates drawn from a \emph{universal gate set}. A widely used example is the \emph{Clifford+T} gate set, which enables the approximation of arbitrary unitary operations to within a bounded error~\cite{nielsen-chuang}.
This also facilitates the analysis of quantum algorithm complexity by providing a standard baseline for comparison.

\subsection{Translating Imperative Programs to Quantum Circuits}
\label{sec:c-to-quantum}

Quantum constraints such as reversibility pose significant challenges when translating a classical imperative language such as C into quantum circuits. 
For instance, consider a generic variable assignment: \texttt{a = b}. While classical programs can freely overwrite variables, this operation cannot be directly implemented in a quantum circuit. Assuming the variables are stored in two quantum registers $\ket{a}$ and $\ket{b}$, we cannot overwrite $\ket{a}$ without first resetting it to a known state, which for simplicity can be assumed as $\ket{0}$. This typically requires reversing the computation performed so far, and it is also consistent with the no-cloning theorem, since the quantum state stored in $\ket{b}$ cannot be copied.
As a consequence, intermediate values generated during the computation must be preserved so that they can later be used for uncomputation. This often requires additional memory to store such intermediate results, implemented in quantum circuits through the use of \emph{ancillary} qubits. 
An example of translating C code into a quantum circuit using reversible uncomputation is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:c-to-circuit}.

The C language provides a rich set of instructions, ranging from simple arithmetic and logical operations to conditionals and loops. Translating this variety of constructs into quantum circuits requires several additional techniques. Since no quantum computing architecture analogous to the classical von Neumann model currently exists—where data is stored in a hierarchically organised memory and instructions are executed by a central processing unit—we rely instead on the quantum circuit formalism, which resembles the behaviour of a combinational network.
A detailed discussion on quantum computer architectures, sequential quantum circuits, or quantum memory implementations is beyond the scope of this work; we refer the interested reader to~\cite{} for a more extensive treatment. Here, we focus on practical methods to implement the most commonly used C constructs within quantum circuits.
For engineering convenience, and without loss of generality, we abstract away low‑level gate details and assume the availability of basic arithmetic and logical building blocks (e.g., adders and shifters) implemented at the quantum register level~\cite{quantumc}. More complex operations, such as those arising in linear algebra, can be decomposed into these fundamental arithmetic components; further details will be discussed in the next section.
When considering purely combinational constructs—where all values are known at compile time—the translation from C code to quantum circuits is often an almost direct instruction‑by‑instruction mapping, provided that reversibility and ancillary‑memory management are properly accounted for~\cite{}. However, many C programs rely on values that become known only at runtime. In such cases, different strategies can be used. If non‑reversible, destructive measurements are allowed, they may be employed to evaluate conditions such as loop termination or branching predicates. If measurements are not permitted, an alternative approach is to fully unroll loops up to a predefined maximum depth and check the condition at each stage of the circuit. Similar reasoning applies to nested conditional branches~\cite{quantumc}.

The MLIR framework was proposed to simplify and unify compiler development in response to the increasing heterogeneity of modern hardware platforms\cite{MLIR_lattner}. This is particularly relevant for domains such as machine learning workloads~\cite{}, homomorphic encryption~\cite{}, online data processing~\cite{}, and quantum computing~\cite{}. By offering a modular and flexible infrastructure, MLIR not only streamlines the design of new compilers but also enables the reuse of optimizations originally developed for other scenarios, without requiring domain-specific knowledge to be built from scratch.
MLIR is founded on the concept of \textit{dialects}, each providing its own intermediate representation, namespace, types, and semantics. Multiple dialects can be combined within a single compilation pipeline, and MLIR supports \textit{progressive lowering} from high-level abstractions to increasingly low-level representations, while ensuring consistency across transformations.
This design allows compiler developers to implement optimizations at the most appropriate level of abstraction, without enforcing a single universal IR. By leveraging the diverse levels of representation, MLIR enables domain-specific knowledge to be embedded precisely where it is most effective. Moreover, as MLIR is becoming a de facto standard for modern compiler infrastructures, it provides access to a wide range of well-optimized dialects for arithmetic and other common workloads, as well as seamless integration with existing frameworks such as LLVM\cite{}.
